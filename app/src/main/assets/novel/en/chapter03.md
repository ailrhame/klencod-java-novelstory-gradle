# Sacrifice for the Company
## Phase 1: Garage in Calasiao

## Chapter 3: Hands in the Garage

At five in the morning, Enzo Santiago opened the door of the garage behind their house in Calasiao. He was greeted by the smell of rust, grease, and barako coffee his mother had left on the table outside. Two e-trike chassis lay on the cement, their wheels touching, with almost no room to pass between them. A bicycle, an old oil drum, and a bag of cables were crammed against the side wall.

Enzo looked at the two chassis and sighed. It was clear the two units would not fit in a garage that was three meters wide. One would have to wait outside, under the mango tree, and every afternoon the rain threatened.

"You're up early, pare." It was Joel Mercado's voice from the doorway. He was holding a thermos and wearing an old t-shirt with an oil stain on the chest. "You haven't even eaten breakfast and you're already working."

"I couldn't sleep," Enzo replied. "I've been thinking about how we can fit the second unit in. It really won't fit."

"It will. We just take the welding machine outside and move the workbench to the other corner." Joel set the thermos down on the workbench and looked over the frames. "But if it rains, we can't do anything outside. So we can't rely on the mango tree."

Enzo laughed softly. In the two months they had been building, he had learned that when Joel got serious, a new problem was sure to need fixing.

The problem was real. They had only one welding machine, a drill with a broken chuck, and a grinder they had borrowed from Joel's father's shop in Malasiqui. When they used the grinder, they couldn't use the drill. When they welded, they had to turn off the garage lights because the power surged and the wires in the wall heated up. Enzo had been worried more than once that something in the house might catch fire.

"We need three things," Enzo said as he wrote on the back of an old receipt. "Space, tools, and money."

"Money is the hardest," Joel replied. "Space, we can manage. Tools, we can still borrow. But money, you can't just borrow that easily."

Enzo fell silent. He knew Joel was right.

* * *

Around eight o'clock, Richard Santiago arrived from the shop. His face was tired, and there was still grease on his fingers that soap could not remove. He hadn't even sat down before he asked what was new with the e-trike.

"Did Mang Ben try it out?" he asked.

"Yes, Tay," Enzo answered. "The battery problem is solved. The unit no longer tips over. He ordered two."

Richard sat down on a plastic chair. For a moment he said nothing. "Two," he repeated. "How much do we need for two?"

"About one hundred and forty thousand, Tay. That includes the battery, motor, controller, and frame. As far as I know, we've already spent P35,000 on the first unit, and that doesn't include the new parts."

"Grace said it has already gone over P35,000."

"Yes, po. She's right."

Richard said nothing more. He went to the kitchen and made himself coffee. When he came back out, he was carrying his cellphone, and there was a number on the screen of someone he hadn't called in a long time.

* * *

The next day, Enzo went with his father to Binmaley. Tita Lorna, his father's sister, lived there and owned a small rice mill and a fertilizer store. In town she was known as someone who was quick to say yes, but slow to say the word "utang" (debt).

They sat across from each other at a table in the small office of the rice mill. The electric fan was on, and the smell of freshly milled palay drifted in through the window.

"How much do you need?" asked Tita Lorna.

"About eighty thousand, Tita," Enzo answered. "For the batteries and the parts for the second unit. I will pay you back in full."

"When?"

"Within one year, po. We'll sell the units to tricycle operators, and we'll earn money once someone places an order."

Tita Lorna looked at Enzo's father. "Richard, did you tell this child that it was this easy?"

"No, Ate," Richard answered softly. "All I said was that we should study it carefully."

Tita Lorna nodded and opened the drawer. She took out a bundle of cash wrapped in a rubber band. She set it on the table and pushed it toward Enzo.

"Eighty thousand," she said. "I don't need interest. But these are my conditions. One year. If you can't keep up with the monthly payments, tell me right away. Don't wait for me to come to you."

"Yes, Tita. Thank you, po."

"Don't thank me. Consider this my tuition for you. I want to see whether you can actually finish what you've started."

On the way home, the ride was silent. Enzo held the bag of money on his lap, and over and over he counted the expenses in his head. Eighty thousand. It still wasn't enough. He would have to be very frugal, and he would have to make sure not a single centavo was wasted.

* * *

When they got home in the evening, Helen Santiago was already at the table. She had made sinigang with shrimp, Enzo's favorite. She didn't ask about the money. She only asked whether Joel had eaten.

"Not yet, Nay," Enzo said. "He's at his mother's house. He went home early today."

"Why so early?"

"I don't know yet."

Helen looked at her husband, but Richard said nothing. Instead, she set the ladle down in Enzo's bowl and then sat down beside him.

"Anak," she said quietly, "I know this project matters a lot to you. But don't forget to eat. The e-trike won't be finished if you're the one who wastes away."

Enzo didn't answer. He only smiled and sipped the broth. Deep down, he knew his mother was right. But he couldn't bring himself to stop either.

The following day, Grace Dela Cruz started a notebook she called the "expense log." She went to the garage with her laptop and laid out the receipts on the workbench, which had paint stains on it.

"These are the rules," Grace said as she looked at the curling pieces of paper. "Nothing gets bought unless it's on the list. Every receipt goes into the log on the day it's paid. And all money goes through me or Tita Helen before it's spent."

"Cousin, you're like a bank," Joel joked as he arrived.

"Better to be like a bank than like a beggar," Grace replied without looking up.

* * *

Two days before the incident, Joel was welding a bracket for the battery tray. It was a small piece, but it had to be strong, since the battery would be heavy once it was full. Joel wore a mask and gloves, but he had forgotten to roll up the sleeves of the sweater he wore under his t-shirt.

Suddenly, a hot spray of metal flew onto his hand. Joel cried out and let go of the electrode holder. When he took off his glove, there was a small cut bleeding on the back of his palm, and a burn along the edge of his thumb.

"Pare! Pare!" Enzo shouted, and he immediately grabbed water and a clean cloth.

"I'm fine, I'm fine," Joel said, wincing. "This has happened to me three times already. It's just that this time it hurts more."

They took Joel to the health center in town. The nurse stitched up the cut and wrapped his hand in a bandage. She told him not to use his hand for three days and not to lift anything heavy.

"Three days?" Joel complained in the car. "So what about the bracket? What about the battery tray?"

"I'll take care of it, po," Enzo answered, looking at the road.

"Can you?"

Enzo didn't answer right away. When they got back to the garage, he turned on all the lights and looked over the parts lined up on the floor. There was no one else left to do the work but him.

* * *

During those three days, Enzo hardly left the garage. He woke up at four, brewed coffee, and went straight back inside. He cut the pipes, cleaned the wires, and sorted the screws that had been piled in a biscuit tin for ages.

He made two mistakes. Once, he forgot to reverse the polarity of a cable, and the controller almost burned out before he noticed the smell of smoke. Another time, he drilled a hole in the frame that was too big, so he had to make a new piece from scrap steel.

On the second day, Mang Ben stopped by the garage to check on their progress. He was riding an old motorcycle and wearing a yellow hat that sat loosely on his head.

"How are the two units coming along, Enzo?" he asked, looking at the parts on the cement.

"The first one is almost done, Mang Ben. The second one needs another week."

"One week?" Mang Ben smiled. "I thought you told me two weeks."

"Two weeks for both, po. But since Joel got hurt, it will take a little longer."

Mang Ben nodded and looked at the cables hanging from the wall. "As long as you're honest about the timing, I won't complain. What matters is that if something breaks, I know who to call."

After he left, Enzo sat down on a small stool and looked at his hands. There were calluses on his palms now, and a small burn on his wrist from the metal spray. For the first time, he felt the weight of the work he had taken on.

* * *

On the third day, Grace arrived with her laptop and a thick folder. Her face was angry, but her voice was calm.

"Enzo, there's a problem with the log."

"What problem?"

"We paid Lim Battery Supply twice for the same shipment. Twelve thousand. There are two entries on the receipts, same date, same amount."

Enzo's ears turned red. He grabbed his cellphone and immediately called the supplier in Dagupan. Before Mr. Lim could even start speaking, Enzo was shouting.

"Why did you double-charge us? Can't you count?"

There was silence on the other end. Then Mr. Lim answered calmly. "Sir, two payments came into our account. But each one has a name on it. The first came from the cash handed over by your companion. The second came from the GCash sent from your account."

Something seemed to strike Enzo in the chest. He took the folder from Grace and flipped through the receipts. He saw his own handwriting in the margin of one paper, a number he had written wrong, and a check mark beside it that he himself had put there.

"Wait a moment, Mr. Lim," Enzo said quietly.

He ended the call and looked at Grace. His cousin didn't answer. She only pointed to a line in the log, where the date of the GCash payment Enzo himself had sent was written, two days before the cash that the other companion had handed over.

"The supplier didn't make the mistake," Grace said. "You did. You paid twice because you didn't see that it had already been paid."

Enzo sat down on the garage floor. For a while he couldn't speak. He remembered the night he had rushed to Dagupan to deliver the list of parts, and the exhaustion he had felt on the road.

"I'm sorry," he said at last. "I was angry, but I was wrong."

"I didn't tell you this to embarrass you," Grace replied. "I just want us to stop looking for someone else to blame. When something is wrong, we fix it. That's all."

Enzo called Mr. Lim again, and this time his voice was calmer. He apologized and asked whether the twelve thousand could be credited toward their next order.

Mr. Lim chuckled softly. "These things happen, Sir. What matters is that you pay correctly. I'll just deduct it from the next shipment."

After the call, Grace and Enzo were silent. Outside, the rain poured onto the mango tree and dripped off the garage roof.

* * *

Three days later, Joel arrived. His hand was still bandaged, but he was smiling. He carried his small toolbox and a pack of cigarettes he hadn't opened yet.

When he walked into the garage, he stopped short. The battery tray was already in place under the frame, and the bracket he was supposed to have made was there too. The welding was even cleaner than his first version, though there were a few small scratches along the edge.

"Who welded this?" Joel asked, clearly surprised.

"Me," Enzo answered, a little embarrassed.

"You've never done that by yourself before."

"No, I haven't. I just remembered what you told me back then. I held the electrode more tightly and slowed my movements."

Joel smiled and lifted the bandaged hand. "Pare, I think I'm going to need to rest a lot longer."

"You can't," Enzo said. "We still need your help with the wiring and the brake system."

"We really do need everything," Joel murmured. He sat down beside the workbench and looked at the parts lined up on the cement. At last, he said what he had been wanting to say for a long time. "Two units in two weeks. Can we do it?"

Enzo took a deep breath. He thought about everything that had happened: the debt to Tita Lorna, Joel's hand, the wrong payment that had brought him back to reality, and his mother's worry at the dinner table.

"We can," he answered. "But we need a clear list. What we're buying, when it arrives, and who is doing the work."

"You handle the list," Joel said with a smile. "I'll do the welding. Grace will handle the money. And Tatay Richard will handle delivering the meals."

The two of them laughed.

* * *

That afternoon, the first shipment of batteries arrived from Dagupan. A small van stopped outside the fence, and the driver got out carrying two boxes stamped with the name Lim Battery Supply. Enzo took the box and opened it to check the contents.

Three batteries were packed securely in foam. The count was complete, and the terminals were intact. Enzo picked one up and felt its weight in his hands, like the weight of a promise.

"Everything's in order," the driver said. "Just sign the receipt, po."

Enzo signed the receipt, and for the first time, he made sure the number written on it was correct. He looked at it twice before handing it back to the driver.

Back inside the garage, he wrote two words on the white board hanging on the wall: TWO UNITS. TWO WEEKS.

Joel stood behind him, leaning against the door with a cup of coffee in hand. Grace was sitting on the bench with her laptop open, writing up a new log. Richard was looking out of the kitchen window, quietly watching the garage.

Enzo still didn't know whether they could manage everything. The money still wasn't enough, and the space still wasn't enough. But for the first time, it felt as though the weight was not resting on him alone.

He took the marker, uncapped it, and began drawing a line beneath the two words. On the line below, he wrote the date of that day and the first task for tomorrow.

*End of Chapter 3.*
