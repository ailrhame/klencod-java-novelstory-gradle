# Sacrifice for the Company

## Phase 1: Garage in Calasiao

## Chapter 5: The Cooperative in Urdaneta

It was only five in the morning, and Enzo was already awake. He had not slept properly since the cooperative called. He lay in bed thinking about the numbers, but each one kept changing in his mind. He had first thought it was ten units. It turned out they wanted twenty.

He got up and went to the garage. The e-trike they had brought out yesterday was still there, parked beside two old tricycles that his father was having repaired at the shop. He wiped the seat with an old rag and checked the battery underneath. The condition was good. The battery that had blown up before had already been replaced, and the date it was installed was still written on the small sticker on the cover.

Joel came into the garage before six. He was carrying two cups of coffee from the store at the corner.

"You still haven't slept?" Joel asked, handing him one cup.

"I slept. Only about three hours," Enzo answered. "I'm thinking about how we'll deliver the twenty units to Urdaneta."

"We don't even know yet how we'll make twenty. Let's go there first and listen. We're not signing anything yet."

Enzo nodded. Joel was right, but he could feel the weight of this day. The cooperative in Urdaneta was the biggest opportunity they had gotten so far. The orders from Mang Ben and the two other tricycle operators in Calasiao were small compared to this.

---

Around seven, the e-trike was in the back of Joel's van. They had removed the front mirror so it would not break during the trip. Grace sat in the passenger seat, holding a folder full of receipts, a list of parts, and a spreadsheet she had typed on her laptop the night before.

"Enzo, before we go in, we need to talk about the price," Grace said as they drove toward Urdaneta. "We haven't yet discussed how much we'll charge per unit for them."

"Our price for a tricycle operator is ninety-five thousand," Enzo answered.

"Yes, but this isn't an ordinary buyer. It's a cooperative. If we give them the same price, they may think we aren't making a big profit. We need to find out whether we can afford a discount and whether we'll still have something left over."

Grace opened the laptop and showed the calculations. Three columns were on the screen. The first was the material cost per unit: battery, motor, controller, tires, steel for the chassis, and paint. The second was the labor cost. The third was the income if they charged the ninety-five thousand price.

"If it's twenty units, three hundred and ninety thousand is the income for labor and materials, if we don't spend anything else," Grace said. "But we have other costs that aren't included here. Warehouse rent, electricity, transportation, and Joel's wages if he's the one doing the work."

"I'm not going to pay myself a wage right now," Joel said from the steering wheel. "As long as there's money to buy parts, I'm fine."

"It's not that simple, Joel," Grace answered. "You also need to eat."

Joel said nothing. He just looked at the road and kept driving.

---

They arrived at the cooperative office around eight-thirty. It was an old two-story building near the Urdaneta public market. A large tarpaulin was hung in front: "Urdaneta Credit and Consumer Cooperative, For the Livelihood of Every Member."

They were greeted by Patricia Salazar, the chairperson of the cooperative. She was in her early fifties, wearing a gray blazer and holding a tablet. Beside her was Mark Dizon, the treasurer, who had thick glasses and a small notebook.

"Welcome, Engineer Santiago," Patricia said as she shook his hand. "You're not an engineer yet, are you? The person who called me said you're only nineteen."

"Just Enzo, please, Ma'am," Enzo answered, a little flushed. "I'm not an engineer yet. I'm a mechanic, and what we make is e-trikes."

"Good. I like people who are honest about what they say," Patricia said. "Come in and show the board the product."

In the conference room, seven board members were already seated. Some wore polo shirts with the cooperative's logo, and others were just in t-shirts. Two women were holding notebooks and ballpens. They seemed to be the ones recording what was discussed.

Joel took the e-trike out of the van and set it down in front of the building, under the mango tree. Several employees came out and looked. A man came closer and touched the tire.

"Is this the one made in the garage?" he asked.

"Yes," Enzo answered. "From our garage in Calasiao. The first unit we made is still there. Mang Ben Villanueva, a tricycle operator in Calasiao, was the first to use it for one week. You can ask Mang Ben yourself if you like."

"We've already spoken with Mang Ben," Patricia said from the doorway. "He called me yesterday. He said the engine is quiet and electricity is cheaper than gasoline. He said he hasn't complained even once."

Enzo smiled. He had not expected Mang Ben to call the cooperative. He remembered the nervousness of the first week of the test, when Mang Ben had worried the vehicle might break down in the middle of the road.

---

They went into the conference room and the presentation began. Enzo showed photos of the e-trike, the battery diagram, and the list of specs. It could travel about forty-eight kilometers on a single charge at normal speed. Charging took five hours using a standard outlet. It came with a three-year warranty on the motor and one year on the battery.

The board listened quietly. After the presentation, Mark Dizon asked a question.

"Enzo, for the battery warranty, where do you get your cells?"

"From a supplier in Dagupan, Sir. We haven't bought large quantities yet. Before, we only bought three or four sets a month."

"If we buy twenty units, can your supplier handle that?"

Enzo stopped. He had not yet asked the supplier that.

"I haven't asked yet, Sir. But I'll contact them right away."

"Good. Because if you run out of batteries in the middle of an order, we are the ones who will be caught in trouble with our members."

Enzo nodded. He felt heat on his neck and face. Grace noticed his reaction and joined the conversation.

"Sir, we have a list of suppliers and two alternatives in Pangasinan and in Manila. We won't depend on just one store." She handed a paper to Mark. "The delivery times and the price of each one are also there."

Mark read the paper. He stared at it for a long time before setting it down.

"The list is in order," he said. "I didn't expect you to bring something like this."

Grace smiled slightly, but she did not say anything.

---

Patricia was the next to ask questions. She sat up straight and placed the tablet on the table.

"This is what the cooperative wants," she said. "Twenty units are needed by our members in three towns. They will use them to carry passengers in the barangays that have no proper bus service. Payment will be made within ninety days from the signing of the contract. We will not pay in installments. Payment will be in full after delivery and inspection."

"Ninety days, Ma'am," Grace repeated while writing in her notebook.

"Yes. And there are two conditions," Patricia said. "First, we need an official receipt for every unit and for every part you purchase. We don't accept payments without documents. Second, we need a contract signed by you and signed by your company. I will not accept a verbal agreement."

The conference room was silent for a moment. Enzo looked at Joel. Joel looked at the floor.

"What do you mean by our company?" Enzo asked. "We don't have a registered company yet."

"That's exactly why you need to register," Mark answered. "We won't sign a contract with three friends who have no name on paper. If you want a large order, you have to become a legal business."

Enzo felt the weight of what Mark said. He knew it was true. But registration cost money, required paperwork, and had to be handled with the BIR and the municipal office. They had none of that yet.

"We understand, Ma'am," Enzo said. "We'll study it and come back before noon."

"All right," Patricia said. "I'll give you one hour. If you can answer, good. If not, another manufacturer called us yesterday."

---

They left the conference room. In the hallway, Grace sat on a bench and opened her laptop. Joel stood by the window, leaning against the wall.

"Okay, we need to decide," Grace said. "For twenty units, we need forty battery packs. Each pack costs about eighty-five thousand if bought in full. That doesn't include the motor and controller."

"How much money do we have right now?" Joel asked.

"Thirty-eight thousand five hundred, after paying for all the parts we bought last month. That's what's left of Tita Lorna's loan after the batteries and the paint."

"Only thirty-eight thousand?" Joel said. "That's a lot of work for twenty units."

"We haven't spent everything yet," Grace answered. "But yes, we need a lot. And twenty won't fit in the garage. Only three would fit if we don't touch Tito Richard's shop."

"That's why we need a warehouse," Enzo said.

He walked over to them and held out his cellphone. The list of warehouses in Calasiao that he had seen on Facebook yesterday was open on the screen. One of them was next to the national road, close to the market. It had two floors, a small office in front, and space for vehicles. The rent was twelve thousand a month.

"Twelve thousand a month," Grace said. "If we pay for three months before the contract ends, that's thirty-six thousand. And there's a deposit on top of that, usually two months."

"So we'd need about sixty thousand just for the warehouse," Joel said. "That doesn't include the batteries."

"Yes," Grace answered. "We're short, Enzo. We can't do it alone."

---

They went back to the conference room before noon. The board was still seated and waiting. Enzo stood up and took a deep breath.

"Ma'am Patricia, Sir Mark, for now we can make twenty units within ninety days, but we need help with funding and space," Enzo said. "We are not yet registered. We will complete the registration within two weeks. If you agree to this schedule, I will sign the contract."

Patricia did not answer right away. She looked at Enzo from head to toe, as if measuring what he had said.

"I don't need promises that you'll explain yourselves," she said at last. "I need a contract that you are able to fulfill. The board will think about it. We'll get back to you tomorrow at ten."

"Yes, Ma'am," Enzo answered.

---

When night fell, they were home in Calasiao. They had dinner at the kitchen table. Helen had cooked sinigang with shrimp, Enzo's favorite, but he barely touched it.

"Eat properly," Helen said as she placed another cup of rice in front of him. "Grace said you didn't have lunch."

"Yes, Ma. I'm full already."

Helen looked at Grace, then at Joel. She knew the kids were hiding something from her.

After dinner, Enzo went out to the back veranda of the house. Grace and Joel followed him. They sat on plastic chairs and looked at the garage, where the light bulb above the door was still on.

"I think we should accept it," Enzo finally said. "Twenty units. Even though it's hard, this is our biggest opportunity."

"Accept how?" Joel asked. "With whose money? Our money is only thirty-eight thousand. It doesn't include the warehouse, the batteries, the motor, or the wages of the workers."

"We can borrow," Grace said. Her voice was soft.

Joel looked straight at her. "From Tita Lorna? We can't bother her again. We've only just paid the first loan, and that loan isn't finished yet."

"I'm not saying from Tita Lorna," Grace answered. "But there are other ways. I'll look into whether we can apply for a small loan from the cooperative itself. If they sign a contract, they already trust us. Maybe they can also help with the funds."

"That sounds nice," Joel said. "But if we can't pay it back, it's not just us who will lose. It's our families, Tita Lorna, Mang Ben too. As your father says, Enzo, when a business fails, the family is the first to pay."

Enzo could not answer. He remembered what his father had said last week, when they argued about using the shop. His father said he was not against the business, but he did not want the name of Santiago Auto Repair ruined by experiments.

"I know, Joel," Enzo said. "I'm scared too. But if we don't try now, we may never get a chance like this again."

Joel was silent for a long time. They could hear the crickets in the yard and the drone of a motorcycle passing on the road.

"I'm not agreeing because I want to lose money," Joel said at last. "I'm agreeing because I've seen how you work in the garage. But I have one condition. No loan that we don't know how we'll pay back. Before we borrow again, we need a clear list of payments and dates. I don't want to repeat what happened with the first loan."

"I agree," Grace answered. "I'll make a schedule tonight."

"All right," Enzo said. "Tomorrow, we'll talk to Ma'am Patricia again."

---

The next day, they returned to the cooperative at nine-thirty. Grace brought the paper with the payment schedule, the list of suppliers, and a copy of the draft receipt they would use. Joel brought the old notebook that contained the details of each part and the time spent on repairs.

Patricia and Mark were already there again. They had arrived earlier than Enzo expected, and they already had a printed contract with them.

"We discussed it with the board last night," Patricia said as she handed over the contract. "We accepted your offer at the price of ninety-three thousand per unit. Two thousand is the discount for a bulk order. Payment will be in full, within ninety days from the signing."

"Thank you, Ma'am," Enzo said, but he had not yet read the contract.

"Read it first," Mark said. "We don't take back our word if you see something wrong. But once you sign, you are responsible."

Enzo read the contract slowly. Each page had details about delivery, warranty, and the penalty if the order was not completed on the set date. On the third page, it was written that there would be a penalty of two percent of the value of each undelivered unit for every week of delay.

"Ma'am, there's a penalty if we're late," Enzo said.

"Yes. That's standard for all suppliers," Patricia answered. "If you want to reduce it, you can talk to the board, but we won't remove it entirely."

Grace looked at the schedule she had brought. She checked the dates and the amount of the penalty. If there was a one-week delay, sixteen thousand would be lost. If it was two weeks, thirty-two thousand.

"Sir Mark, if there's a one-month delay because of the batteries, how many pesos is the penalty?" she asked.

"Twenty-one thousand six hundred, if twenty units are not delivered at the same time," Mark answered. "But if the delivery is staggered, it's lower."

"In that case, we'll deliver gradually. Five or six units every two weeks. It will also be easier for the cooperative to inspect each batch."

Mark thought for a moment. "I agree to that, as long as it is written in the contract and each batch has a receipt."

---

Joel read the contract beside Enzo. He was quiet as he read each paragraph. He stopped at the part about the battery warranty.

"Twelve months for the warranty," Joel said. "But if one battery fails, who pays for the cost?"

"The company," Patricia answered. "You are responsible for all defects that are not caused by the member's misuse."

"In that case, we have to make sure each battery is correct before we deliver it," Joel said. "We can't afford to make mistakes at this size."

Patricia looked at Joel with a slight look of respect. "Your cousin says you're the one who installs every wiring. Is that true?"

"Yes," Joel said. "I'm the one who installs it. I don't let other people do it."

"Good. That's what we want to hear."

---

Before signing, Enzo and Grace stepped out into the hallway first. They had a short conversation.

"Enzo, are you sure?" Grace asked. "Once you sign, we can't take it back."

"I know," Enzo answered. "But if I don't sign now, the order will go to another manufacturer. We're not ready yet, but I'd rather we be the ones fighting the problem than let someone else take the opportunity."

"This isn't about pride, is it?"

"No. It's about whether we can do it. If we can't, we'll admit it to them. But we'll try first."

Grace nodded. "After this, we need to register right away. I'll handle the paperwork. You handle the supplier and the warehouse."

"All right."

They went back to the conference room. Joel was already seated by the window, looking outside. Enzo walked to the table and picked up the ballpen.

Before he signed the contract, he looked at Patricia. "Ma'am, I'm going to sign. But I want you to know that we will do everything to make this happen."

"We know," Patricia said. "That's why we agreed."

Enzo signed at the top of the page, next to the name of the cooperative. Grace followed as a witness. Joel also signed, even though the contract did not require him to. He said he wanted everyone who worked on the first unit to have signed.

Patricia extended her hand to Enzo. Her grip was firm.

"Good luck, Enzo," she said. "I hope I won't regret this."

---

On the way home in the car, everyone was quiet. Grace held a copy of the contract on her lap, wrapped in a plastic folder. Enzo sat in the passenger seat, looking out the window at the rice fields that were beginning to turn yellow as the harvest approached.

"We need to rent a warehouse this week," Enzo finally said. "The one near the national road. Two floors. The top floor can fit ten units, and the batteries can go on the ground floor."

"And we need to buy batteries from Dagupan before the end of the month," Joel added from the steering wheel. "Otherwise, other orders will go ahead of us."

"We also need a small factory," Grace said. "The garage won't fit us anymore. If we want to make twenty in ninety days, we need a place with a work line. We can't keep building them one by one behind the house."

Enzo looked at her. "A factory?"

"Yes. Our first factory. Just a small one for now. One line for assembly, one station for testing batteries, and one area for painting. It won't be as big as the large plants, but it won't be a garage anymore."

Enzo did not answer. The word "factory" seemed too big for three people who had started in a small garage. But on the other hand, he knew Grace was right. If they didn't do this now, they wouldn't be able to handle twenty, and certainly not the orders that would come after.

When they reached Calasiao, Enzo got out of the van and stood in front of the garage. He opened the door and walked in. The e-trike was still parked in the middle, beside the battery boxes, tires, and steel that had piled up over several weeks.

He looked around. The space was small. The inside was hot, and the air smelled of grease and paint. On the small table at the side lay old receipts and the list of parts, papers that had been gathered over three years of his struggle.

He took a deep breath. They did not yet know whether they could manage the entire project. The funds were short, the space was short, and time was slipping away quickly. But he now had a contract in his hand, a promise that had to be kept, and people who believed in them.

He walked out of the garage and closed the door. Behind him, the small sign that Grace had written during the first month was still hanging: "Factorual – Garage of Hope."

He looked at the sign for a long time. Then he murmured to himself, barely loud enough for anyone else to hear.

"Can we do it?"

No one answered. The only sounds were the hum of cars on the road and the chirping of crickets in the yard.

They would have to find the answer in the weeks to come.

*End of Chapter 5.*
