# Sacrifice for the Company

## Phase 1: The Garage in Calasiao

### Chapter 1: The Garage in Calasiao

The first thing he remembers whenever he opens the small door of the garage behind their house is the smell of grease and hot rubber. No one knew then that in that very corner would begin a company that would make its mark on the whole world, but for Enzo Santiago, it was simply a place where he had work he wanted to do and time he could spend.

He was nineteen years old that autumn. He was thin, had a scar through one eyebrow from being hit by a tricycle when he was a child, and always wore an old T-shirt with the logo of a company that had gone bankrupt long ago. His whole life had been lived in the town of Calasiao in Pangasinan. He was born there, he grew up there, and there he learned that every broken machine has a reason it stopped.

In the workshop of his father, Richard Santiago, Enzo learned almost everything he knew about machines. The workshop was small. Three light posts stood at the edge of the road, and a tarpaulin read *Santiago Auto Repair: Walang Iniiwang Sira* ("We Leave No Breakdown Behind"). Inside, there were two hydraulic lifts that worked only now and then, old tools, and a radio that was always tuned to an AM station that broadcast nothing but news about the price of rice and gasoline. When Enzo came home from school, he went straight to the workshop and helped his father with motorcycles, tricycles, and the occasional jeep that neighbors brought in.

The biggest difference between Enzo and his father was the question Enzo always asked.

"Why is it built this way?" he asked once, while opening the engine of an old Honda.

"Because that's how it was always done," his father answered. "As long as it runs, don't touch it."

But Enzo could not accept that answer. He wanted to know why one part of the engine ran so hot, why certain parts always failed first, and whether all of this could be made easier, cheaper, and sturdier. At nineteen, he already carried a small notebook full of drawings and calculations, which he called his "dream sketches." Some of those sketches were nothing more than childish doodles. Others were unusual and far smarter than anyone around him expected.

There in the garage, beside the old compressor and the boxes of broken parts, he started a project that no one imagined was serious.

---

One rainy October afternoon, Joel Mercado arrived. Joel came from the next barangay and had been working for two years at a mechanic's shop in Urdaneta. He was tall, broad-shouldered, and spoke slowly but moved quickly. He was known all over town because his hands could fix any machine, as long as he had enough time and patience.

"Why are you here?" Enzo asked, bent over an old tricycle motor.

"I heard from Mang Ben that someone was building a vehicle back here," Joel answered. "I thought, who would build a vehicle in a repair shop garage? So I came to see if it was true."

Enzo smiled. "It's true. But it's not a vehicle yet. It's more like a vehicle that doesn't run yet."

Joel laughed and sat down on an old stool. He studied the chassis made of steel and aluminum, the two wheels that were not yet the same size, and the battery strapped to the back. At the front, a thick cable came out of a box made of old plastic.

"What's that electric thing?" Joel asked.

"Electric," Enzo answered. "No gasoline. Just a battery."

Joel stared at the machine for a long time. He did not speak right away, but the mix of worry and interest on his face was plain to see.

"Why do you want to build something like this?" he finally asked.

Enzo took a deep breath before answering. This was the question he had long wanted to answer honestly.

"Because I saw Mang Ben," he said. "Every morning, he works on his tricycle from four in the morning until ten at night. Half of what he earns goes to gasoline, and the other half goes to repairing the engine. When the engine breaks down, there's no income. I said there has to be a way to avoid this. Without gasoline, it costs less to run. A simpler engine breaks down less. If it breaks down less, people get to take home more money."

Joel did not answer right away. He looked around, at the boxes, the cables, and the old computer on the table with its three colored lights. Then he nodded slowly.

"In that case," he said, "I'll help you. But we need a few more people."

---

The second person to enter the story was Grace Dela Cruz, Enzo's cousin on his mother's side. She was studying Accountancy at a college in Dagupan and often came home on weekends. Her voice was small, but her eye was sharp, and she loved organizing lists and papers even when no one asked her to.

One Saturday, Grace arrived at the garage carrying coffee in a tumbler and a thick folder.

"Nanay Helen said you're here again," she told Enzo. "She said you're not eating. Did you even bathe?"

"I bathed," Enzo answered, even though he hadn't.

Grace looked around. "What are you working on now?"

"A vehicle."

"Electric?"

"Yes."

Grace set the folder on the table and opened it. It was full of receipts, bills, and papers with numbers written on them.

"What's that?" Enzo asked.

"The total of everything you've spent on the garage this month," Grace answered. "I got it from Tiyo Richard. I didn't tell you because I knew you'd be upset. But there are costs here you shouldn't have to pay alone."

Enzo could not speak. He had no idea his cousin had noticed every peso he took out of his own pocket. The batteries he bought in Dagupan, the cables from Manaoag, the steel he ordered from a supplier in San Carlos. Everything was written down.

"How much is it all?" he asked.

"More than thirty-five thousand," Grace answered. "And that's before the electricity."

Enzo's eyes went wide. It had been barely a month since he started, and the money he had saved from fixing the neighbors' motorcycles did not even reach half of that amount.

"How am I going to pay for this?" he whispered.

"You don't have to pay for it alone," Grace said. "If you treat this as a business, there has to be income coming in. If not, it's just expenses."

She looked directly at Enzo. "If you're serious, you need a name, proper papers, and a plan. You can't just say 'I'm just building things.'"

Enzo thought it over. After a moment, he spoke.

"Factorual," he said.

"What?"

"The name of the company. Factorual." He took a sheet of paper and wrote the word down. "From *factor*, meaning a contributing element. Then *actual*, meaning real. What I mean is the factors that drive the real world. Not just dreams. Real."

Grace studied the paper for a long time. Then she smiled slightly.

"It's a heavy name," she said. "But it's a good one."

---

That evening, they held a meeting at the small table inside the house. Enzo, Joel, and Grace were there. Nanay Helen was cooking sinigang in the kitchen, and they could hear the clink of a pan as Enzo spoke.

"Our first product," said Enzo, "is the e-trike. An electric tricycle, for operators in town. It doesn't need to be beautiful. It only needs to run, save costs, and be easy to repair."

"I wonder how much they could afford to buy?" Grace asked.

"I don't know yet. First we need to build one that runs properly. Then we ask."

Joel nodded. "I'll take care of the engine. I can make the motor and the battery control sturdier. But I need one thing."

"What?"

"A test ride. I want to see how the motor heats up after two hours of driving. Driving around the garage isn't enough."

Enzo was silent for a moment. He knew they needed a real road and a real load. But every test ride came with risk, and not every risk was one he could take on himself.

"This Friday," he said. "On the road to Sta. Barbara. If it works, we'll move to a longer route."

Nanay Helen came in carrying a bowl of sinigang. She looked at the three young people hunched over papers and sketches.

"What is it you're talking about now?" she asked, with a voice full of both weariness and love.

"A vehicle, Nay," Enzo answered.

"A vehicle again," his mother murmured, but she was not angry. She set the bowl on the table and stroked her son's hair. "Eat something before you go inventing all kinds of things. People don't live on cables alone."

Grace laughed. Joel laughed too. But Enzo did not laugh. He only stared at the bowl, thinking about the figures written on the paper and the numbers that were still unfinished.

---

On Friday morning, they took the first e-trike out onto the road. The sun was already hot over Calasiao, and the air smelled of rice fields and the fish stall they passed. Joel sat in the driver's seat, his eyes fixed on the small screen mounted on the handlebar. Enzo sat in the back with a laptop connected to the sensors. Grace stood at the side of the road with a stopwatch and a notebook.

"Ready?" Enzo shouted.

"Ready!" Joel answered.

Joel pressed the pedal. The e-trike moved off quietly, quieter than any tricycle the people of town were used to. No smoke. No clatter. The only sounds were the soft hum of the motor and the tires gliding over the asphalt.

The three of them laughed. But when they had gone past three kilometers, the alarm on Enzo's laptop suddenly sounded.

"What's that?" Joel shouted.

"The motor is overheating!" Enzo answered. "Stop for a moment!"

Joel pulled over to the side of the road. All three of them got out. When Enzo touched the motor, it was hot, but not so hot that it would be damaged right away. He checked the sensor, looked at every number, and saw that the temperature rose quickly whenever the road went uphill.

"We need to change the cooling," Joel said. "We could add aluminum fins around the motor. Then we put in a small fan."

"Isn't that too heavy?" Grace asked.

"It's a little heavier, but it won't break down."

Enzo nodded. For the first time, he felt an unusual excitement. This was no longer a simple dream. There was a problem, and people were working together to think of a solution.

But while the three of them stood at the side of the road, a man riding a tricycle passed by. He stopped right in front of them and rolled down his window.

"What's that frightening vehicle?" the man asked, with a mix of surprise and a bit of pride. "No sound, no smoke, like a ghost."

It was Mang Ben Villanueva, the tricycle operator the Santiago family had known for a long time. He had been driving around town for years and was known for his loud voice and sharp tongue.

"Mang Ben!" Enzo shouted. "It's working now!"

"Working?" Mang Ben laughed. "All right then, start it up in front of me."

Joel looked at Enzo. Enzo took a deep breath before nodding.

---

Mang Ben got into the e-trike and drove it a short distance, going to the barangay corner and back. On the first lap, he said nothing. On the second, he asked about the speed and how far it could travel before the battery had to be charged. On the third, he got off and stared at the vehicle from head to tail.

"How much?" he asked directly.

Enzo did not answer right away. He had not yet thought through the price in full. All he knew was that it was cheaper than a gasoline tricycle and that there would be less to repair.

"We don't know the exact amount yet," he answered. "But if you could buy it, you'd save on gasoline and on engine repairs."

Mang Ben shook his head, but not in anger. "In that case, maybe I could be your first customer. But I need to see how it performs over the course of one week. If it breaks down in the middle of the road, you're the one I'll come after."

"Yes, sir," Enzo answered, and he could not tell whether a smile or fear showed on his face.

That night, he did not sleep well. He lay on the old bed in his room, staring at the ceiling, and thought about every number he had seen on the laptop. He remembered what Grace had said about the costs. He remembered what his mother had said about eating. And he remembered Mang Ben's voice saying, you're the one I'll come after.

He got up and took out the small notebook. On the first page, he wrote the name of the company and the date:

*Factorual. Started in the garage in Calasiao. October 7.*

Then, underneath, he wrote:

*First task: build a vehicle with a real customer.*

He looked at those two lines for a long time. The words seemed so small, but for him, every letter carried a weight he did not yet fully understand.

Outside the window, he could hear the chirping of crickets and the faint sound of his father's radio, still tuned in at the workshop. He stood up, left the room, and went down to the kitchen. His mother was there, seated at the table, holding a cup of coffee and staring into space.

"Haven't you gone to sleep yet?" she asked when she saw him.

"Not yet, Nay."

His mother did not answer. She stood up, took a piece of pandesal from the container, and handed it to him.

"Eat," she said. "No matter what you're doing, you still have to eat."

Enzo bit into the pandesal. It was still warm, and its taste was familiar, like home, like a life he could not yet bring himself to leave.

He did not know then that this night was the beginning of everything. He did not yet know that the small garage behind the house would become a factory, that the notebook would become a book of contracts, and that this pandesal would be one of the things he would find hardest to leave someday.

All he knew that night was that he had built a vehicle, that a customer was waiting, and that there was a family who loved him even though they did not want to understand every detail of his dream.

For a nineteen-year-old, that was enough to begin.

---

*End of Chapter 1.*
