# Sacrifice for the Company

## Phase 1: Garage in Calasiao

## Chapter 4: The First Payment

It was only five in the morning, and Enzo Santiago was already awake. He had not slept well. Grace's question from the night before was still on his mind: "Enzo, once Mang Ben takes the two units, how much will actually go into our pockets?"

He had not been able to answer right away. Now, standing in front of the garage and looking at the two e-trikes lined up on the cement, he knew the answer. Not a single peso had gone into their pockets yet. All he knew was that they still had debts to pay.

Joel Mercado came out from the small shack behind the house, carrying a rag and a thermos of coffee. He looked like he was still short on sleep.

"Are the two of them ready?" Joel asked.

"They're ready. But my question from yesterday isn't finished," Enzo replied. "How many months before we earn back what we spent?"

Joel sat on the edge of a small stool and poured coffee into an aluminum cup. "If your question is when we'll start earning, I can't answer that. But if your question is whether the vehicle works, yes. It works. We tested it, and Mang Ben even rode it."

"I know. But working and getting paid are two different things."

Joel didn't answer. He knew his friend was right.

---

Around eight, Grace arrived carrying her laptop, a notebook, and a plastic folder stuffed with receipts. Her hair was tied back, and she clearly had come from another long night of studying. She was an Accountancy student in Dagupan, but during the break she was practically living in the garage.

She laid the papers on the wooden table under the umbrella. "This is the total cost so far," she said. "One hundred sixty-two thousand, nine hundred thirty-nine pesos spent on the two units, including the batteries, motors, tires, and parts. This doesn't include electricity or Joel's time."

"Joel's time isn't included?" Enzo asked.

"No. Because if we include that, this paper would only show a loss. So I'm keeping it separate for now."

Joel laughed. "Thanks, Grace. Separate turns out to be scarier."

Grace opened the laptop and showed them the spreadsheet. It had three columns: costs, payments, and balance. The payment was the P45,000 that Mang Ben had handed over when the first unit arrived at the operator's workshop in Calasiao. In total, the balance was still negative P117,000.

"This is all we've collected," Grace said. "The P45,000 is only for the first unit. The second unit hasn't been paid yet, because Mang Ben has only promised to pay after one month of operating it."

"Why is it short?" Enzo asked. "You said earlier that our price was right."

"The price is right, but there's one cost we subtracted that we didn't think about before." Grace pointed to a line. "The delivery expense. We needed Tito Ding's van to bring the units to Calasiao. That was two trips. There's gasoline, and he has to be paid."

Enzo closed his eyes. He hadn't thought about the cost of transport. All he had been thinking about was the motor, the design, and the wiring.

"So the truth is," Grace continued, "if we count every cost for both units, including delivery and the first operator's payment, we're short by almost a hundred thousand pesos. The payments we've received don't even reach half of the total cost."

"Do we have to pay Tita Lorna right away?" Joel asked.

Grace looked at Enzo. "Not right away. But we need to have a plan. The agreement says we make monthly payments starting from our first earnings. If we don't earn anything, we'll have to explain ourselves."

Enzo didn't respond immediately. He remembered Tita Lorna's face when they signed the P80,000 loan papers. She was calm, but every word she spoke carried weight. "I know a family is trusting you," she had said back then. "Don't let us down."

---

A few days before that, the two units had been delivered to Mang Ben Villanueva. He was the tricycle operator who had tried the e-trike during a one-week trial, and he was the first to place his trust in Factorual. Now he was operating two units on the Calasiao-to-town route.

During the first month, the two vehicles ran smoothly. The motors were quiet, they charged quickly, and the controllers didn't overheat. Mang Ben was thrilled. Whenever he passed by the garage, he would roll down his window and shout, "Enzo! I'm earning more than I ever did before!"

But in the third week, a call came in on Enzo's phone.

"Enzo, it's Ben," said a voice on the other end. The road noise was loud. "The second unit won't run for long anymore. The battery drains fast. Before, it lasted a full day of operating. Now it's only half a day."

"Where are you now?" Enzo asked, already standing and looking for his motorcycle keys.

"We're at the market. Can you come?"

"I'm coming. Don't run it for now."

Enzo got on his motorcycle and hurried to the market. When he arrived, Mang Ben was sitting in the e-trike's seat, wiping sweat from his forehead. Beside him, two passengers were already waiting in line.

"Sorry, Enzo," said Mang Ben. "I didn't mean for this to happen. But I have to earn a living."

"I understand," Enzo replied. He opened the battery compartment and checked the wires. Inside, some cells were swollen and there was a smell of burning. It wasn't serious, but the problem was obvious.

He straightened up and took a deep breath. In his head, he was already counting. A new battery would cost around P38,000. The labor, the delivery, and Joel's time were not even included. If he charged Mang Ben, the first operator would lose his trust. But if he didn't pay, the vehicle might stop altogether and Mang Ben would lose his income.

"Mang Ben," Enzo said. "I'll fix the battery. It's free. Leave it to me."

Mang Ben looked up at him. "Free? Then who's going to pay?"

"The company will, sir. I'll take responsibility for the cost."

"Enzo, I don't need you to shoulder everything."

"Not everything. But the problem with that unit came from us. We have to fix it."

Mang Ben nodded, but he still looked hesitant. "Alright. But when you're done, call me so I can test it again. If it works, we're good."

---

When Enzo returned to the garage, Joel was already prepared to open the battery. The cells, multimeters, and an old pair of tweezers that Joel had used to solder the previous unit were laid out on the table.

"What did you find?" Joel asked.

"Three cells are swollen. We don't know how many more will follow." Enzo sat down beside the table. "I still need to call the supplier in Dagupan. Do we have a warranty on these cells?"

Grace opened the folder and searched for the contract. "We do. Six months. But there's a condition. It requires the right charger and the right operating temperature. If not, the warranty is void."

"So that means Mang Ben made a mistake while using it?" Joel asked.

"I don't know yet," Grace replied. "We need to check the charging log. We can't say whether it's his fault or ours."

They fell silent. Enzo knew the right answer was to find out the truth first. But right now, time was the problem. Mang Ben's route couldn't stop for long.

"Alright," Enzo said. "We'll try the warranty. But if it doesn't cover it, I'll pay the difference from our own earnings."

"Enzo, we haven't earned anything yet," Grace said quietly.

"I know. That's why someone has to earn something."

---

By the afternoon, the battery repair had begun. It took three hours. They replaced the swollen cells, added new insulation, and tried charging it slowly. Nothing exploded, and everything went smoothly. But even after two hours, the unit was still quiet and didn't overheat.

That night, a message came in from the supplier. The warranty was not complete. It only covered the three cells, not the entire pack. The remaining cost would have to be paid by Factorual.

Grace read the message out loud. Then she quietly set down her phone.

"How much?" Enzo asked.

"Sixteen thousand. Including delivery."

Enzo said nothing. But the next morning, he went to his father's workshop.

---

If there was one thing Richard Santiago could not stand, it was having the workshop used for a project he knew nothing about. He had run Santiago Auto Repair in front of their house for many years. The shop had been around for twenty-five years, and nearly every motorcycle in the barangay had passed through his hands. Every part, every drop of oil, every screw, he knew exactly where it belonged.

But over the past few weeks, the workshop's appearance had changed. The steel frames of the e-trike blocked the walkway. There were cables on the floor. Joel had used the workshop's welding machine because it was more powerful than the one in the garage. And the neighbors' motorcycles, which should have been repaired the previous week, had been left on the side because there was no space and no time.

When Richard saw the vehicle blocking the walkway and the motorcycles left sitting there, his anger exploded.

"Enzo!" he called from the workshop doorway. "What is this?"

Enzo stepped out of the garage. "Father, I'm sorry. We just needed some space."

"Space? In my workshop? My neighbors are waiting for their motorcycles. Their child needs to get to work tomorrow. And you, you're using my welding machine for your own toy?"

"The project is not a toy."

"No? Then why am I the one paying for the workshop's electricity? Why am I the one worrying about how many more vehicles will be delayed?"

Joel came over from the back, still holding a tool. "Tito Richard, I'm the one who asked Enzo to use the welding machine. It's not his fault."

"Joel, don't blame yourself," Richard replied. His voice was quieter now, but his stare was even more intense. "I know you two are capable. But the workshop has its proper schedule. It's not a place for experiments."

Helen Santiago came from the kitchen holding a ladle. She looked at the father and son, then at the steel bars scattered across the floor. "Richard, that's enough. Talk about it later."

"No, Helen. I have to fix this now."

Helen said nothing more. But Enzo saw the worry on his mother's face. He knew this wasn't just about the workshop. It was about money, about family, and about his father's fear that they might lose everything they had worked so hard for.

Richard turned and went back into the workshop. Enzo heard the roll-up door slam shut.

---

After lunch, Enzo decided to move the steel bars to the back of the house. He and Joel, along with a cousin named Marco, handled the move together. They were sweaty and exhausted, but everything was in order before dark. The welding machine was returned to the workshop with a promise that it would not be used again without permission.

Afterward, Enzo went back to the workshop and sat outside, beside the old bench his father used whenever he talked with customers.

Around seven in the evening, Richard came out. He was carrying two bottles of cold water. He handed one to Enzo and sat down at the other end of the bench.

They were quiet for a long time. The only sounds were the chirping of crickets and the occasional tricycle passing on the road.

"How old are you?" Richard asked.

"Nineteen, Father."

"Nineteen." Richard nodded. "When I was nineteen, I was already opening my own shop. It wasn't as big as this one. It was more like a warehouse. But it was mine."

"I know."

"You don't know how heavy it is to think about money when you don't have your own yet."

Enzo didn't reply. He drank his water and watched the crickets gathering around the light of the lamp post.

"Enzo," Richard continued. "I don't want to ruin our workshop. It's what supported us. It's what put you through school. And if it's lost because of a project we don't even know will make money, I won't be able to bear it."

"The workshop won't be lost, Father."

"How can you say that?"

"Because if we make mistakes, I'll be the one who pays. Not the workshop."

Richard looked at him. For a long time. As if he were measuring every word.

"You said you would pay."

"Yes."

"How much?"

"I don't know yet, Father. But for now, the company still owes the battery warranty. Sixteen thousand. I'll take care of it. I won't use the workshop for that."

Silence fell again. It lasted until Richard took a deep breath.

"I don't agree with what you're doing," he finally said. "Not completely. But if you treat this as work and not a game, and if the workshop's schedule isn't affected, then go ahead and continue with it."

Enzo stood up, unsure how to respond. "Thank you, Father."

"Don't thank me. Take care of yourself. And don't forget your family while you're working so hard for the company."

Enzo nodded and smiled slightly. The conversation was not perfect, but something had changed. His father no longer looked at him with anger, even though he still didn't fully agree.

---

The next morning, before work even began in the garage, Grace sent a text. The message was short but heavy: "Enzo, someone called our office. I need to pass it on to you."

Enzo called back. A few seconds passed before someone answered.

"Good morning. This is Celia Ramos, general manager of the Urdaneta Transport Cooperative," said a clear-voiced woman. "We read about your e-trike in a local group of operators. We'd like to know if you're ready to accept a larger order."

Enzo froze. He looked at Joel, who was standing beside the e-trike holding a wrench. Joel looked back at him, silent, but with a question in his eyes.

"How big is the order?" Enzo asked, trying hard to keep his voice steady.

"We don't know the exact number yet. But we're looking at about twenty units for our members in Urdaneta and Rosales. We'd like to see a demo, talk with your operators, and learn the price of the batteries and the service."

Twenty units. In Enzo's mind, numbers raced by. Twenty units, a huge investment. Bigger batteries, more parts, more people. More debt. Greater opportunity.

"Ma'am Ramos," Enzo said. "Can we come to your office in Urdaneta this week?"

"Yes, of course. What is your earliest day?"

Enzo glanced at Grace again. She was standing at the garage doorway, holding her laptop, her brow furrowed. Joel looked at the e-trike, then back at him.

"Tomorrow," Enzo answered. "We'll come tomorrow."

After he hung up, the whole garage fell silent. Then Grace was the first to speak.

"Twenty?" she asked.

"Twenty," Enzo replied.

Joel let out a soft laugh and shook his head. "Enzo, we need to think this through carefully. We can't handle twenty without additional capital."

"I know," Enzo said. He looked at the two e-trikes lined up on the cement, at the wires hanging on the wall, at the old welding machine that had been returned to his father's workshop. "But this is the first time someone has asked when we can deliver. Not who's paying us."

The question now was how they would get the money for twenty units, while they still owed P117,000 and the P16,000 battery warranty. And how they could keep their family's trust while the company grew.

Enzo didn't have the answer yet. But for the first time, he could see a path, even if it was long and still unclear.

*End of Chapter 4.*
