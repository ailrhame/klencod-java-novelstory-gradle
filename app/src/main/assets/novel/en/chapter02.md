# Sacrifice for the Company

## Phase 1: Garage in Calasiao

## Chapter 2: The One-Week Trial

The sun had not yet risen when Enzo Santiago woke up. It was only five o'clock, but the garage light was already on. From his room, he could hear the faint sound of an electric drill, steady and tireless. Joel Mercado again, he thought. It looked like his friend hadn't slept either.

Enzo came down in his tsinelas with a towel hanging over his shoulder. At the garage door stood Joel, holding a coffee in a plastic cup. His hands were covered in grease, and there was a rust scar on his arm that Enzo had never noticed before.

"Pre, did you sleep?" Enzo asked.

"Two hours." Joel pointed at the e-trike parked in the middle of the garage. "But look at this before you complain."

Enzo walked over and looked at the vehicle. Before, its metal body had been bare, gray and smooth. Now, thin aluminum fins were attached all around it, packed tightly together like the teeth of a comb. Behind the motor, a small electric fan was fastened to an old bracket with wire.

"You made this?" Enzo asked.

"You too. You're the one who decided, so if it fails, it's your fault." Joel smiled, tired but happy. "I cut the aluminum from the old window frames at your father's shop. I had Ka Nestor at the welding shop in Poblacion bond it. I also didn't let my cousins sleep last night, because they were the ones holding the pliers while I attached the bolts."

Enzo did not answer right away. Joel was telling the truth. For three nights, they had worked after classes and after their jobs at the shop. On the first night, it was nothing but measurements and sketches on paper. On the second, Joel spilled half a liter of oil on the floor, and they had to scrub the cement until ten o'clock. On the third, they built the first real version of the cooling fins, but it was too heavy and blocked the moving part of the engine.

Now, it looked like there was hope.

"Can it run?" Enzo asked.

"We're not going to start it yet. First we need a test ride, and I need to see whether it still gets hot after ten minutes. If it does, we're no different from yesterday." Joel set his coffee down on the workbench. "Have you eaten?"

"Not yet."

"Go to the kitchen. You might find Mom cooking tinapa. She's been asking since earlier whether you're still alive."

Enzo laughed. On the other side of the yard, the kitchen was already open. He could smell the sinangag and the tomatoes sauteed in garlic. Helen Santiago, his mother, stood at the stove with a towel on her shoulder, the way she always did in the mornings. She didn't turn around when Enzo came in, but she spoke right away.

"How long have you been out there?"

"Just in the garage, Mom."

"In the garage. You were like that last week too." She set a plate on the table. "Enzo, I'm not against what you're doing. But you should also learn to eat and sleep. The work doesn't finish just because you didn't eat."

"Yes, Mom."

Enzo sat down and ate quickly. Then his father, Richard Santiago, came out of his room, already in slacks and a white polo shirt, ready to go to the shop. He looked at his son chewing his sinangag.

"I've been hearing that drill since earlier," Richard said. "What's happening with the motor?"

"It has fins now, Dad. We put in a fan. We're testing it this morning, if Joel comes with me."

Richard nodded, but he didn't smile right away. Enzo knew that look. His father didn't want to show hope, because he was afraid his son would get hurt if it didn't succeed. Enzo also knew his father as a man who worked at the shop from seven in the morning to six in the evening, and who counted every peso spent on the parts they bought.

"How much have you spent so far?" Richard asked.

Enzo froze. The truth was, he hadn't opened the notebook again, the one where Grace Dela Cruz recorded every expense.

"I haven't checked it completely yet, Dad."

"Check it before you go on the test ride," Richard said, grabbing the motorcycle keys. "I'm not angry about spending money. What I don't like is spending it without knowing where it went."

---

Grace came to the house around eight o'clock, carrying her backpack and a laptop with a Dagupan City Library sticker on it. She was Enzo's cousin and a third-year Accountancy student at a private college in Dagupan. Since the project started, she had been the one keeping all the receipts, the money, and the list of everything they had bought.

She sat down on the long bench by the garage and opened the spreadsheet.

"There," she said. "Here are the real numbers. The total we've spent up to yesterday is over P35,000."

"Does that include the electricity?" Enzo asked.

"Not yet. Electricity is separate. We've been charging the battery pack every night for two weeks, and I saw Tito Richard's bill from last month. It went up by almost P1,800 compared to before." Grace pointed to a row on the screen. "I think our real cost is bigger than you're imagining."

Joel listened in silence. He wiped his hands on a rag before he spoke.

"Can we include the electricity in the unit price? So we know the real cost of each e-trike?"

"We can. That's why I want us to look at this properly." Grace turned the laptop toward Enzo. "This is what I've been telling you from the start. It's not enough that the engine works. You also need to know how much is coming out of our pockets for every day of testing."

"Yes, I know," Enzo replied. "That's why we need the test ride today. If we see that it runs well, then we can think about the price."

Grace stared at him as if she still wanted to argue, but she only nodded.

"Fine. But for the next expense, write it in the notebook first before you buy. I won't be an accountant if I can't follow my own system."

Joel laughed. "Poor you, Enzo, having a cousin like that."

"I'm used to it," Enzo said.

That was how the name Factorual began, from a joke by Grace. When Joel asked her what should go on the company papers, she said the project shouldn't be described as just an experiment. The truth was, they needed to know the "factor" of every peso they spent, and they needed to compare it with the "actual" that happened on the road. So she suggested Factorual. It wasn't serious at first, but Enzo kept repeating it until it stuck.

---

Around nine o'clock, they rolled the e-trike out of the garage onto the road in front of the house. Joel sat in the back with a multimeter, an infrared thermometer, and a small notebook. Enzo was at the handlebars. Grace climbed in at the back, carrying a stopwatch and her cellphone for pictures.

"Don't go fast," Joel reminded him. "I want to see the heat first when it's moving slowly."

"Slowly," Enzo answered.

He started the motor. It made a faint sound, almost like a sewing machine, but it was quieter than a tricycle with a motorcycle engine. The vehicle moved along the Calasiao road and passed in front of the public market. Some vendors looked up and asked whose strange tricycle it was, the one without an exhaust pipe.

At the first kilometer, the ride was smooth. At the second, the motor's temperature rose slightly according to the thermometer. At the third, it dropped again. It was not as hot as the earlier test ride a week before, when they could almost smell the paint burning.

"So far, so good," Joel said, looking at the display. "But this still isn't the real situation. In real operations, it's not just fins and a fan. It also has to handle a heavy load, the heat of the sun, and stopping and starting over and over."

"That's why I'm offering it to Mang Ben to try," Enzo answered.

Mang Ben Villanueva was a tricycle operator in Sta. Barbara. He had been driving passengers for a long time, from the town center to the surrounding barangays. He was known for being selective. He would not ride any new vehicle without testing it first and asking others about it. The first time he saw Enzo's e-trike, he asked only one question.

"How many times have you crashed?"

"None yet, Mang Ben. But it hasn't gone through a long run yet."

"That's where many young people go wrong," Mang Ben had said. "They think that if the engine looks good in the garage, it will be good on the road."

Then he rode the first test ride to Sta. Barbara, with a sack of rice in the back. When he got off, he gave no compliments. He only said one thing they would never forget.

"I'll take it if your e-trike doesn't crash for one week."

Crashing was what Enzo least wanted to hear. On the provincial roads, even a small mistake in weight or brakes could cause an accident. Joel said that before the trial, the whole vehicle had to be checked first. So for the next two days, Joel hardly left the garage except to buy parts.

---

That night, Joel showed the last change to the cooling system. He removed the old rubber that trapped heat around the motor and replaced it with a looser housing with holes on the side to let air flow through. They also added extra thermal paste to the part where the motor touched the fins.

"This isn't magic," Joel said. "Heat moves. If you don't help it get out, it stays inside here and gets hotter."

"I understand," Enzo answered. "That's why there's no fan in other people's cars."

"There's none in their cars because their cars are bigger and more air passes through them. Ours, we have to make our own air."

They both laughed. They were tired but overjoyed, because for the first time, they could see that the work was headed in the right direction.

In the kitchen, Helen was peeking out the window. She watched the two young men laughing in the middle of the garage, with grease on their faces, water on the table, and bolts scattered on the floor. She said nothing. But before she went to bed, she left a plate of rice and viand on the table, covered with another plate so the flies wouldn't land on it.

---

Mang Ben's trial began on Monday morning. Their agreement was simple. For one week, seven days, the e-trike would be used for Mang Ben's route in the town of Calasiao and the nearby barangays. He would drive at his usual hours, from six in the morning to six in the evening, and ordinary passengers would ride. Joel would inspect the vehicle every night. Grace would record the income, the expenses, and the distance. Enzo would look after the motor and the battery in the garage.

"I don't need favors," Mang Ben said before leaving. "I need real numbers. If I earn, you earn too. If not, tell me right away."

"Yes, Mang Ben," Enzo answered.

After the e-trike left the corner, Enzo followed his father's motorcycle to the shop. He couldn't tell Richard he was nervous, but his father obviously already knew what he was feeling.

"That man is quiet, but he doesn't lie," Richard said as he opened the shop's shutter. "If he said he'd try it, he'll really try it."

"I hope so, Dad."

"Don't blame yourself if something breaks. The job of the person who builds a vehicle is to learn when something breaks."

On the first day of the route, everything went smoothly. On the second day, one passenger complained because the vehicle moved too slowly going up the bridge. On the third day, Mang Ben arrived at the garage at seven in the evening, carrying a plastic bag of lumpia and looking tired.

"Enzo, there's a problem with the battery," he said right away, before he even got off.

Joel came over quickly. He touched the side of the battery pack and winced.

"It's hot," he said. "This isn't normal."

They took the battery pack out of the frame and carried it to the table outside the garage, where a light bulb hung. According to Mang Ben, the battery pack got hot whenever it charged for more than two hours. It hadn't been ruined yet, but there was a smell of heat and a little smoke from the connector earlier, before he arrived.

Grace asked how many hours they had been charging each night. Enzo checked the notebook and saw the same answer for all three days: seven and a half hours, because they had been using the old charger given to them by a store at the market.

"Seven hours?" Joel repeated. "But this battery can only take less than that."

"That's why," Enzo said. "We tried to speed up the charging on the second night so Mang Ben wouldn't run out in the morning, so I opened the small housing to let it cool, but I didn't put in anything to remove the heat."

Joel didn't need to get angry. He just nodded quietly and picked up the pliers. They fixed the connector, replaced the charger with a more suitable model that Grace bought in Dagupan the next morning, and fitted a temperature sensor to the battery pack. They also made a small rule: the charging would not last more than five hours, and it would stop once the battery reached a set temperature.

"Is the charger expensive?" Mang Ben asked.

"More expensive than before," Grace answered. "But that's a cost we can't ignore. If the battery burns, we'll lose much more."

Mang Ben looked at the three of them and nodded. "Right. That's why I don't want just promises."

After the repairs, they put the battery pack back in. Joel drove the e-trike around the garage for ten minutes while a thermometer was attached beside the motor. The temperature stayed at a safe level. There was no smell of heat, no smoke.

"Solved," Joel said. "But this isn't the end. Tomorrow, there will still be things we don't know."

---

The fourth to sixth days went the same way. Mang Ben left early, came back at noon to recharge, and came back again before dark. Many passengers asked about the vehicle. Some said it was quieter than a tricycle with an engine. One vendor said the e-trike smelled brand new, not like gasoline smoke that hurts your throat.

But some people also complained. On the bridge outside the town, the tire slipped a little when it was slightly wet. At one corner, the battery dropped faster than Grace had expected. Joel said this was normal in the first week of the trial, but they could not ignore it.

"That's why there's a trial," Enzo said while Grace calculated the distance and income.

"Yes, but remember," Grace replied, looking at her laptop. "The trial is to find out what needs fixing, not to prove that we're already perfect."

On the sixth night, as Mang Ben drove back to the garage, he stopped outside and looked at the yard.

"It runs well," he said. "Better than my tricycle. But there's one thing I want to tell you."

Enzo came over, nervous.

"I'm earning more this week than in an ordinary week. It's not because of luck. I carried more passengers because the route in town is faster, and there are fewer things that cause heat on the road. I'm saving more on gasoline, and I haven't paid for any repairs because no one crashed."

"Thank you, Mang Ben."

"Don't thank me yet. The week isn't over." He smiled slightly. "But if this goes through, I want two units."

The air in the garage seemed to stop. Joel stared at Enzo. Grace stood holding her laptop, and she glanced at the screen to confirm the number in front of her.

"Two units?" Enzo asked, almost in a whisper.

"Yes. One for me, and one for my son Rodel, who wants to operate in the other barangay. But I won't ask you for a favor. I'll buy them when there's a fair price and proper papers."

"Mang Ben, we don't know yet how much we can give as the price."

"I know. That's why I'm only telling you now. I want you to know you have a chance." He stood up and adjusted his hat. "Let's finish the seventh day. After that, we'll talk about the numbers."

---

The last day of the trial arrived. The sky was clear in the morning, but there were clouds in the west that suggested rain in the afternoon. Enzo woke up early and went to the garage. Joel was already there, sitting on an old chair and staring at the e-trike.

"I couldn't sleep," Joel said.

"Me neither."

"If we crash today, everything we've done will lose its value in other people's eyes."

"If we don't crash, it still has value for us," Enzo answered. "That's what matters, not what other people say."

Joel didn't speak again, but he nodded.

Around six in the morning, Mang Ben arrived, earlier than usual. He looked at the e-trike from the side, pressed the brake, and checked the tire. He started it slowly, then rode and circled the garage twice. He said nothing.

"Mang Ben, is there a problem?" Enzo asked.

"No. I'm just thinking about how I'll explain it to my companions when they ask."

"What will you explain?"

"That it's quieter, that it pulls harder going uphill, and that it takes less money out of my pocket." He paused. "But I won't say it's perfect. There are still gaps. You need to fix the brakes on wet roads, and you need to write down everything that's missing from the papers."

"Yes," said Grace, standing at the back. "It's already written down."

Mang Ben got back on and said goodbye. Enzo and Joel followed at the back on Joel's motorcycle, all the way to the town. They stopped in front of the market and watched the e-trike turn onto the Sta. Barbara road.

All day, they couldn't keep still. Joel kept looking at his cellphone to check for calls. Grace wrote numbers in the notebook, and Enzo walked back and forth in front of his father's shop, peeking inside every now and then.

"Don't you want to rest at all?" Richard asked while changing a tire on an old Toyota.

"I can't sit down, Dad."

"I'm not surprised. I was the same way when I started here." Richard didn't look at his son. "When the day ends, tell me the truth, even if it hurts."

"Yes, Dad."

At five in the afternoon, someone knocked on the shop's shutter. It was Mang Ben, still riding the e-trike, with a smile on his face they had never seen before. Behind the vehicle, three passengers got off and paid with an extra tip.

"No one crashed," Mang Ben said as he got down. "Seven days. No one crashed."

Joel jumped with joy. Grace hugged Enzo, not knowing how to show her happiness. Richard, who had been standing quietly at the back of the shop, took a deep breath and only nodded.

"Mang Ben," Enzo said, barely able to speak. "Thank you."

"Thank you too," Mang Ben answered. "But now, the question isn't whether it works. The question is how you'll make it something many people can do."

He handed an envelope to Grace. Inside was his total income for the week, along with a list of trips and distances that he had written in his own hand.

Grace opened the envelope and read it quickly. Then she read it again, more slowly.

"Mang Ben, is this more than what you usually earn in a week with the tricycle?"

"Yes. Almost a quarter more, even after paying for electricity and the extra charging. I only saved on gasoline, and that doesn't include the repairs I didn't need." He leaned against the shop wall. "That's why I'm ordering two."

Everyone fell silent. Enzo heard the sound of the electric fan on the motor, still turning even though the engine was off, as if it had a life of its own.

Joel moved beside Enzo and whispered, "Pre, two units."

"I know."

"We can't do that now. Our money isn't enough for even one unit that's finished, with the right charger, the right battery, and the right papers."

"I know that too."

Grace looked at the laptop, at the spreadsheet full of numbers. They had already spent over P35,000. Add the electricity, the charger, the aluminum, the welding, and the parts that broke during the first week of the trial. That still didn't include Joel's unpaid time or Richard's old motorcycle that they had used for some parts of the trial.

"If we build two units," Grace said, "we'll need funds to buy parts in bulk. We'll need capital for batteries, for equipment, and for a proper workspace. The house garage isn't enough if we're making two."

"So where will we get it?" Enzo asked.

No one answered right away. Richard looked at his son, then at the house, then at their shop. Helen looked out the kitchen window, holding a ladle, and Enzo felt that she also knew how heavy the question was.

Mang Ben held the e-trike key and smiled slightly.

"If I were you," he said, "I wouldn't ask whether it's possible. I'd ask where the money will come from."

But no one answered that question. All Enzo heard was the sound of the fan on the motor, turning, and the faint sound of rain starting to hit the garage roof.

*End of Chapter 2.*
